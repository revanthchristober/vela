# VELA — brand foundation

> Fictional brand created for a self-initiated concept build. No part of this describes a
> real company, product or customer.

**Blueprint refs:** §1 (product brief), §5.1 (visual direction), Phase 1

---

## 1. Positioning

**VELA makes a small, considered range of skin and body care for people who want their
daily routine to feel like something.**

Most of the premium D2C wellness market sits at one of two poles. Clinical brands lead
with percentages and pipettes; they are trusted but joyless. Lifestyle brands lead with
mood and marble; they are beautiful but vague, and shoppers quietly suspect they are
paying for the photography.

VELA sits between them on purpose: **the calm of the lifestyle brands, the specificity of
the clinical ones.** Short ingredient lists that are printed, not hidden. Textures
described in words a person would actually use. Nine products, not ninety.

### Category

Premium daily skin and body care, India-first, ₹1,200–₹4,500.

### One-line positioning

> For design-conscious people who find most skincare either clinical or vague, VELA is a
> nine-product range built around daily rituals — short ingredient lists, textures worth
> the counter space, and no claims we cannot print on the bottle.

### Brand promise

**Modern rituals, engineered for everyday life.**

"Engineered" is doing real work in that line. It is the permission slip for specificity:
it lets VELA talk about formulation, ratios and testing without becoming a lab brand, and
it quietly mirrors what this project itself is — something crafted rather than assembled.

### What VELA is not

- Not a "clean beauty" brand. No fear-based ingredient marketing, no "free from" lists.
- Not a wellness-as-identity brand. No self-optimisation, no discipline, no before-and-after.
- Not a range that grows every quarter. Nine products. New ones replace, not accumulate.

## 2. Audience

**Primary:** design-conscious D2C shoppers, roughly 22–40, metro India, mobile-first.
They discover brands through creators, saved Instagram posts and search. They read
ingredient lists — not because they are chemists, but because a brand that hides one is
telling them something.

**What they are actually solving for**

| Surface want | Underneath it |
| --- | --- |
| "Something that works for my skin" | Not wanting to think about it every morning |
| "Nice packaging" | Not wanting to hide the bottle when someone visits |
| "Natural / clean" | Wanting a short list they can pronounce, not a moral position |
| "Worth the price" | Wanting to know why it costs ₹1,800 and not ₹400 |

**Objections, in the order they occur**

1. *"Is this just packaging?"* — the premium-price suspicion. Answered by ingredient
   transparency and texture specificity, never by shouting.
2. *"Will it suit my skin?"* — answered by naming who each product is *not* for.
3. *"Will it actually arrive, and can I return it?"* — answered by plain shipping and
   returns copy near the buy button, not buried in the footer.
4. *"Which one do I start with?"* — answered by the ritual sets and by one honest
   recommendation per product page.

**Voice of customer — the language to mirror**

"sits well under sunscreen" · "doesn't pill" · "not sticky" · "runs out too fast" ·
"smells like nothing, in a good way" · "I hate the pump" · "does it break you out"

## 3. Voice

VELA sounds like **a knowledgeable friend who is not trying to convince you.**

Calm, specific, quietly confident. Short sentences. Concrete nouns. It describes rather
than persuades, and it trusts the reader to draw the conclusion.

### Five rules

1. **Describe the thing, not the feeling it should produce.** "A gel that turns to milk
   on contact" beats "a luxurious cleansing experience."
2. **Every claim is checkable.** If it cannot go on the bottle, it does not go on the
   site.
3. **Name the trade-off.** Every product page says who it is not for. This buys the right
   to be believed everywhere else.
4. **No exclamation marks, no superlatives, no urgency theatre.** Nothing sells out in
   four hours here.
5. **Sentence case everywhere except the wordmark.** Title Case Reads Like An Ad.

### Register by surface

| Surface | Register |
| --- | --- |
| Hero and section headlines | Declarative, unhurried, 4–9 words |
| Product names | Plain nouns. `Balance Cleanser`, not `The Balance™ Ritual Cleanse` |
| Product descriptions | Second person, present tense, texture-first |
| Ingredients and benefits | Flat and factual. No adjectives doing sales work |
| Buttons and labels | Verb + object. `Add to bag`, `See the ritual` |
| Errors and empty states | Human, brief, never cute. "Your bag is empty." then a route out |
| Journal | First person plural, essayistic, no SEO padding |

### Do / don't

| Don't | Do |
| --- | --- |
| "Unlock your skin's true potential" | "A cleanser you can use twice a day without tightness" |
| "Clinically proven formula" | "Tested on 40 people over eight weeks. Two reported stinging." |
| "Luxurious, indulgent, transformative" | "Thick, slightly warming, absorbs in about a minute" |
| "Shop Now!" | "Add to bag" |
| "Our revolutionary blend of botanicals" | "Six ingredients. All six are on the front of the bottle." |

### Words VELA does not use

_luxurious · indulgent · pamper · glow up · self-care journey · game-changing ·
revolutionary · clean · chemical-free · toxin-free · miracle · transform your skin_

## 4. Messaging hierarchy

**Level 1 — the one thing:** Modern rituals, engineered for everyday life.

**Level 2 — the three pillars**

| Pillar | Says | Proven on the site by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Short lists** | Six to nine ingredients per product, all of them named on the front | Ingredient block on every PDP, expanded with the role of each ingredient |
| **Made to be used** | Textures and formats designed for a real morning, not a photograph | Texture-first descriptions, "how it wears" notes, refill sizes |
| **A range, not a catalogue** | Nine products that work together, with an obvious place to start | Ritual sets, one honest pairing recommendation per PDP |

**Level 3 — proof.** Ingredient percentages where they matter. Sourcing. Batch and
manufacture dates. Reviews that include the two-star ones.

## 5. Headline options for the homepage hero

| # | Headline | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | **Modern rituals, engineered for everyday life.** | The brand promise, used verbatim. Recommended: it carries both halves of the positioning — "rituals" for warmth, "engineered" for specificity — and it is the line every other surface can ladder back to. |
| B | Nine products. Every ingredient on the front. | Sharper, more differentiated, immediately checkable. Better if the hero has to do the arguing alone; weaker as a brand line because it is an argument rather than a promise. |
| C | Skincare you can read. | Shortest and most memorable. Risks reading as a "clean beauty" swipe, which VELA explicitly is not. Hold as a campaign line rather than the hero. |

**Sub-headline (paired with A):** A small range of skin and body care built around the two
minutes you already spend. Short ingredient lists, printed on the front.

**Primary CTA:** `Shop the range` — names the destination, and "range" reinforces the
nine-not-ninety point. Rejected: `Shop Now` (weak, generic), `Discover VELA` (vague).

**Secondary CTA:** `How we formulate` → the story page. Serves the "is this just
packaging?" objection at the exact moment it occurs.

## 6. Naming conventions

- **Products:** `[Attribute] [Format]` — Balance Cleanser, Hydrate Mist, Recovery Oil.
  One word, then the format. No sub-brands, no trademark symbols.
- **Sets:** `[Occasion] [Kit|Ritual]` — Morning Ritual, Reset Kit.
- **Collections:** plain category words — Daily care, Body care, Rituals.
- **Sizes:** stated in ml on the variant selector, never as "regular / large".

## 7. Visual direction — words the design must satisfy

Editorial, warm, minimal, tactile. Generous whitespace. Photography treated as product
and brand evidence, not decoration — a bottle on a real surface, not a bottle on a cloud.
Strong typographic hierarchy carries the page; colour is nearly absent so that the single
deep green accent reads as intentional every time it appears.

The site should feel **expensive, not busy.** The operational test, applied to every
section in review: *if this element were removed, would the page be worse?* If the answer
is "no, just emptier", it goes.
